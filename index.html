Before you argue the number

Know what you are actually challenging

For most non-agricultural property, Wisconsin assessment law focuses on the property’s market value as of January 1 of the assessment year. A huge percentage increase can be a warning sign, but the size of the increase by itself does not prove the new value is wrong.

Your strongest argument is not “this went up too much.” It is: “The January 1 market value is lower than the assessment, and here is the evidence.”

Wisconsin’s valuation hierarchy matters. A recent arm’s-length sale of the subject property is generally the first and strongest indicator. If there is no qualifying recent sale, recent comparable sales are next. Other valuation methods come after that when adequate comparable sales are unavailable.

That is why the questions “What sales did you use?” and “How did you adjust those sales to my property?” are far more useful than simply arguing about the percentage increase.

The process

How to challenge a Wisconsin property assessment

The safest approach is to treat this like a short evidence case. Keep dates, forms, contacts, sales, photographs, and your requested value organized from the beginning.

Verify the property facts

Ask for the property record card and compare it with reality. Check acreage, waterfront/frontage, square footage, building type, year built, condition, finished areas, outbuildings, plumbing, septic, utilities, seasonal versus year-round use, access, and other property characteristics.

Factual mistakes are often the cleanest issues to resolve because they are not arguments about opinion. Bring photographs and documentation.

Attend Open Book and ask the assessor to show the work

Open Book is your chance to review the assessment roll and informally discuss value. Ask the assessor for the property record card, sales analysis, comparable sales used, and the adjustments that led to the final value.

Useful questions: What recent sales support this land value? Which properties were treated as comparable? How were differences in frontage, lot size, slope, utilities, improvements, seasonal use, access, and condition adjusted? Was there a recent arm’s-length sale of this property? What valuation method was used if comparable sales were unavailable?

Decide your opinion of value

The formal objection form asks for your opinion of the property’s value. Do not stop at “the assessment is too high.” Build a defensible number from the strongest evidence available.

Examples include a recent arm’s-length sale of your property, recent genuinely comparable sales, a professional appraisal, or documented errors in the assessor’s property data.

Give notice of intent to object

Wisconsin DOR guidance states that the owner must give the Board of Review clerk written or oral notice of intent to file an objection at least 48 hours before the first scheduled Board of Review meeting.

Because state guidance notes that the law does not clearly say whether email or fax alone constitutes filing of the objection form, use a method you can document and confirm directly with the clerk.

Complete Form PA-115A in full

The Objection Form for Real Property Assessment (PA-115A) is the state-prescribed form for real property objections. Complete every applicable section, including your opinion of value. DOR guidance warns that an incomplete objection form can lead the Board to refuse to hear the case.

Present the evidence at Board of Review

The assessor’s assessment is presumed correct unless the objector presents evidence showing it is incorrect. Keep your presentation factual and organized. State the assessed value, your requested value, and the evidence supporting that value.

Bring copies of your evidence and be prepared to answer questions under oath. The Board decides based on the evidence in the record.

Evidence beats outrage

Build a Board of Review packet

Anger is understandable. The Board, however, needs evidence. Organize your packet so a stranger can understand your property and your argument quickly.

1. Your assessment documents

  • 2026 changed-assessment notice
  • Prior assessment notice or tax bill
  • Parcel number and legal description
  • Property record card
  • PA-115A objection form

2. Property facts

  • Lot acreage and usable area
  • Water frontage and shoreline characteristics
  • Slope, access, wetlands, easements, or restrictions
  • Building square footage and condition
  • Water, septic, heat, and other utilities

3. Market evidence

  • Recent arm’s-length sale of your property, if any
  • Recent truly comparable property sales
  • Recent appraisal, if available
  • Photos showing meaningful differences
  • Adjustments explaining why a comp is higher or lower
Tip: A nearby property is not automatically a good comparable. For lake property, frontage, water body, lot size, slope, buildability, utility access, septic, structure quality, view, and seasonal versus year-round use can matter enormously.
At Open Book

Ask for records, not reassurance

The Wisconsin Property Assessment Manual describes the assessor bringing the property record card, sales analysis information, and other data supporting the value to the review process. Property owners should bring evidence supporting their own opinion of value.

Write down who you contacted, when, how, whether you received a response, and what records were provided. If you later need to show that information was requested but unavailable, a clean timeline is much stronger than a memory of several frustrating phone calls.

Do not assume silence extends a deadline. If you are waiting for the assessor to respond, continue protecting your Board of Review filing rights anyway.
If BOR does not fix it

There are appeal paths after Board of Review

These routes have different deadlines and consequences. Read the official DOR guide and consider legal advice before choosing among them.

DOR appeal under Wis. Stat. § 70.85

Generally available only if you first contested the assessment at BOR, the BOR value does not exceed $1 million, and other statutory conditions are met.

  • Written complaint due within 20 days after receipt of the BOR determination, or within 30 days of the affidavit date if no return receipt exists.
  • $100 filing fee.
  • DOR may not change a value found within 10% of the municipality’s general assessment level.

Circuit court certiorari

A court can review the BOR record under Wis. Stat. § 70.47(13).

  • Generally must be filed within 90 days after receipt of the BOR determination.
  • The court reviews the BOR record.
  • You cannot introduce new valuation evidence that was not in the BOR record.

Excessive assessment claim

Wisconsin law also provides a municipal excessive-assessment claim route under § 74.37, subject to statutory requirements.

  • You generally must first have appealed to BOR.
  • Taxes must be timely paid.
  • The claim is generally due by January 31 of the year the tax is payable.
  • Other appeal choices can affect eligibility.
If the problem is bigger than one parcel

Wisconsin also has a whole-roll reassessment petition process

Wisconsin’s § 70.75 process is different from an individual Board of Review objection. DOR guidance says property owners may petition for reassessment of a taxation district when the combined assessed value represented by the petitioners is at least 5% of the locally assessed value of the district, and the complaint alleges the assessment roll is not in substantial compliance with law and that reassessment would promote the public interest.

This is a much bigger undertaking, but it is exactly why organized community data can matter when many owners believe the issue is systemic rather than parcel-specific.

FAQ

Questions people are already asking

Does a massive percentage increase automatically make the assessment invalid?

No. The legal question is generally whether the assessed value is supportable under Wisconsin assessment law, including the January 1 market value standard for most non-agricultural property. A dramatic increase can justify close scrutiny, but evidence of value is what matters in a formal challenge.

Can I just show that my neighbor is assessed for less?

That may help identify an issue, but it does not by itself establish your property’s market value. Stronger evidence includes a recent qualifying sale of your own property, truly comparable sales, an appraisal, factual errors in the record card, and evidence showing why the assessor’s comparables or adjustments are not appropriate.

Do I have to attend Open Book before Board of Review?

Open Book is strongly useful because it gives you a chance to understand or resolve the issue informally. The formal BOR filing requirements are separate. Protect your BOR rights even if the assessor has not responded to you.

Can someone else represent me?

Wisconsin DOR provides Form PA-105 for agent authorization. Review the form and BOR instructions carefully if someone will represent the owner.

What if I cannot attend BOR in person?

DOR provides Form PA-814 to request permission to testify by telephone or submit a sworn written statement. The request does not replace the notice-of-intent and objection-form requirements, and the Board must approve the alternative method.

Can we use Barron County’s tax portal to build a public database of everyone’s property?

Be careful. Barron County’s tax-data portal publishes terms that prohibit redistribution of owner names, addresses, and related tax information from that site. This community project is therefore designed to collect owner-submitted information and publish aggregate statistics rather than scrape and republish the County’s tax database.

What happens after people submit data here?

Submissions are kept in the site’s form system for review. The project can use non-identifying submissions to calculate statistics such as median percentage increase, lakefront versus non-lakefront changes, land-value changes, and reported assessor-response patterns. Personal contact information should remain private.
